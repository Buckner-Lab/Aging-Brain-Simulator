Insights and puzzles from decisions required to get the model to work

Each entry carries the date it was captured (YYMMDD), which stays fixed; later understanding is added as new entries.

Resilience falls with age 261010

Normal aging here is a slow linear loss from 25, small next to AD's toll. With aging that small, the AD cascade alone cannot give the oldest old the dementia seen in populations, about 35% of people 90 and older and about 13% of 90–94-year-olds newly each year. In this world it takes a median of 21 years from amyloid positivity to dementia, and amyloid's onset risk doubles only every 16 years, so dementia from AD rises only about fivefold from the late 60s to the late 90s, where populations show a twenty- to thirtyfold rise. More AD risk lifts that curve at every age rather than steepening it: amyloid positivity in unimpaired 50- and 90-year-olds and dementia at 65–69 leave their ranges long before new cases at 90–94 come close, and steeper, stronger LATE or a faster cascade for late onsets fall short too. Yet the pathology is there: half of 90–94-year-olds without dementia are amyloid-positive and a third are past AD's cognitive onset, but only about 2% a year convert. So the toll that AD, LATE and white matter lesions take on cognition doubles every 5 years from 78 (the starting settings): pathology the very old already carry turns into impairment faster, while a pathology-free brain is untouched, which keeps SuperAgers common. It is a calibration device rather than a measured mechanism, and only a narrow band of its two settings meets the checks: dementia at 90+ sits near the top of its range and amyloid positivity among unimpaired 90-year-olds near the bottom, because amyloid-positive people are the ones converting. Autopsy studies find the link between AD pathology and dementia weakens in the oldest old (Savva et al. 2009), so it is best read as reserve running out together with co-pathologies the model does not have (hippocampal sclerosis, arteriolosclerosis, microinfarcts, Lewy bodies) that travel with the pathology it does. It multiplies the level of each toll, so pathology carried for years becomes more impairing as a person ages; it leaves aging, sleep, engagement and brain volumes alone; and it switches off with Resistance and resilience.

261010 The 21 years above holds for the world without the fall; with resilience falling from 78 the median is 18 years. Falling resilience or late-rising aging? sets the fall beside the other way to pass the checks.

261014 Version 88 turns the fall off in the starting world and brings back aging's late rise in its place (Falling resilience or late-rising aging?). Because the fall multiplies the level of each toll, it also speeds decline wherever pathology is present, before diagnosis and after, and after onsets in the 80s it made dementia progress three to four times as fast as in population studies (Early dementia passes too quickly). It remains a setting under Resistance and resilience, off while the toll never doubles.

Falling resilience or late-rising aging? 261010

Two ways of giving the oldest old their dementia both pass all 11 reality checks, so the checks cannot choose between them. In the starting world aging stays a slow linear loss, and from 78 the toll of AD, LATE and white matter lesions doubles every 5 years (Resilience falls with age). The other, the world of version 74, has no fall; instead cognitive aging gets a late rise that doubles every 6 years from 70 (at 70: episodic memory 0.0105, speed 0.0125 and executive function 0.009 SD a year), so aging itself becomes the main cause of decline after 90. Without either, dementia at 90 and older and new cases at 90–94 fall far below their ranges. Both shorten the median time from amyloid positivity to dementia, to 18 years with the fall and 19 with the late rise, against 21 with neither.

The two put similar decline into the oldest old but give it to different people, and that is what would settle it: memory trajectories in the pathology-free oldest old. Among people aged 90–94 without dementia whose amyloid (below 20 Centiloid), tau PET (below 1.30 SUVR), LATE and white matter lesions have not begun, episodic memory falls about 0.03 SD a year with the fall, against 0.17 SD in those with pathology; with the late rise it falls about 0.13 SD a year, against 0.17. Memory slopes in very old people shown to be free of these pathologies, at autopsy in clinical-pathologic cohorts or by PET and plasma markers, would decide. Published findings pull both ways: the common pathologies explain only 43% of the variation in late-life decline (Boyle et al. 2021, Brain), which leaves room for aging, while people who died at 90 or older with both AD and LATE pathology had declined more slowly than those who died younger with both (Kapasi et al. 2020, Neurology), the opposite of a toll that grows with age. The starting settings keep falling resilience until such data decide.

261014 Version 88 switches the starting world to the late rise, at the settings above, with resilience no longer falling. Dementia's course decided it, which the checks did not test: the fall multiplies pathology's toll and so the pace of decline before and after diagnosis, and after onsets in the 80s dementia progressed three to four times as fast as in the Cache County study (Early dementia passes too quickly); the late rise lowers everyone's level alike, without singling out the people with pathology, so the course runs smoothly through diagnosis. All 14 checks pass with it. Among people aged 90–94 without dementia, episodic memory now falls 0.14 SD a year in the pathology-free and 0.18 SD in those with pathology (0.04 and 0.19 with the fall). The test named above would still decide between them.

When does normal decline begin? 261013

Until version 82, normal aging took episodic memory, speed and executive function down from 25 at about 0.015 SD a year, so a 60-year-old had lost half an SD of memory before any pathology, and practice gains in the follow-up study then hid almost all of the decline that remained. That world passed all 11 reality checks, but it builds in one side of an open dispute. Cross-sectionally, memory falls from early adulthood; longitudinally, with practice controlled by new samples, Betula finds no episodic decline before about 60 and traces the cross-sectional fall to cohort differences in education (Rönnlund et al. 2005; Nilsson et al. 2009), as the Seattle study does for most abilities (Schaie 1994). Salthouse (2009) and Whitehall II (Singh-Manoux et al. 2012) find decline before 60, most clearly in speed and reasoning.

The starting world now follows the longitudinal data for memory and keeps early decline for speed: memory and executive function lose 0.03 SD a year from 60, speed 0.018 from 25, and each decade of birth adds 0.15 SD to the level reached, about the size of the Flynn effect. Lifetime normal memory loss by 90 is nearly unchanged (0.9 SD against 1.0); it is moved later. All 11 checks stay in range, falling resilience is still needed for the oldest old (without it, dementia at 90 and older drops to 12%), and the people who differ at each age differ for the same reasons. What changes is what studies see: true decline among 60–89-year-olds with MMSE 24 or more is 0.093 SD a year, practice now hides about three-quarters of it rather than nine-tenths, and the cross-sectional slope among the living aged 60–90 steepens from 0.55 to 0.75 SD a decade. SuperAgers aged 80–89 fall from 25% to 15%, because a reference group aged 50–65 that has lost little sets a higher bar. Both worlds pass every check, so the checks cannot choose between them; within-person change before 60, measured with new-sample practice controls in people free of pathology, would. The earlier world remains a setting away (linear onset 25 for all three domains, 0.015 SD a year, cohort gain 0.10).

261014 From version 88 aging's late rise does the work of falling resilience for the oldest old (Falling resilience or late-rising aging?). It adds to normal loss late in life: in people free of pathology, normal aging has taken about 0.4 SD of episodic memory by 70, 0.8 by 80 and 1.6 by 90, against 0.3, 0.6 and 0.9 without it.

Where are the maintainers? 261013

Version 85 added a reality check for memory maintainers. It applies the rule of Josefsson et al. (2012) to a study run in the simulated world the way Betula was: everyone dementia-free and aged 35–85 in 2011 who is still alive and dementia-free in 2026 is tested every 5 years with an error of 0.4 SD, and a maintainer is someone whose memory changed at least 1 SD more favorably than the mean of their 5-year age group. Betula's attrition-corrected analysis found 18% maintainers and 13% decliners among all 1,558 people tested at least twice, and 21% and 10% among those with complete data; an analysis of complete cases only found 15% and 15%. This world gives 14.1% maintainers, below the range, and 13.5% decliners, close to the published figure. In true values, without the error, it has almost no maintainers (0.0%) and 5.7% decliners: nearly every maintainer the study finds is test error.

The reason is structural. Memory here only falls, from 60 by normal aging and at any age from pathology, and nothing raises it, so most of the differences in change come from the minority with pathology. That long tail of decline widens the spread of change, and no one's change can stand 1 SD above the mean of their peers. Widening maintenance (its spread from 0.30 to 0.80) leaves both shares where they were, since it only slows a loss everyone has. Betula's shares sit near what a bell-shaped spread of change gives once error is added (about 16% on each side), which suggests that real change varies more evenly between people than it does here, with some whose memory holds or rises beyond test error. The page's own maintainer definition is absolute (little loss in the years before 85, set in the SuperAgers and maintainers box) and does find maintainers (16.5% of the living aged 85+); it is the rule relative to peers that finds none. The check is left out of range rather than tuned. The shape of the distribution of practice-corrected memory change in people free of pathology would settle it; a model change worth testing is a rate of change of each person's own that is unrelated to pathology, or gains that differ between people, judged by whether maintainers in true values rise while decliners stay near 13%.

261013: from version 86 the check compares with Betula's complete-case analysis (15% maintainers, 15% decliners), since the simulated study, like that analysis, keeps only people tested at every wave; 14.1% is within its range. The puzzle stands: in true values this world has no maintainers.

261014: version 88 tests the change suggested above. Each person's episodic memory now drifts on its own from 60, unrelated to the brain, pathology or any trait: a random step each year, with an SD of 0.105 SD. With it, 41% of the differences in 5-year memory change at 65–75 are not shared with speed and executive function, close to the 42% of episodic-memory change not shared with a general factor of change in the meta-analysis of Tucker-Drob et al. (2019); without it, 8% were. In true values the world now has 5.6% maintainers and 7.0% decliners; as measured, 14.6% and 14.3%, against Betula's 15% and 15%. A fixed slope of each person's own, tried first while resilience still fell with age, matched the same short-term variety but piled it up over the years: memory at 85 spread 40% wider than at 60, SuperAgers rose from 13% to 25%, and one dementia-free person in five gained memory over ten years. Steps that start afresh each year leave memory at 85 as widely spread as at 60 (SD 1.04 at both; 0.98 at 85 without them), and one dementia-free person in twelve gains memory from 65 to 75 (none without them). With aging's late rise, back in version 88 (Falling resilience or late-rising aging?), everyone loses more memory in their 80s: SuperAgers by the Northwestern rule are 13.7% of the dementia-free aged 80+ (19.2% with falling resilience instead), and the page's absolute maintainer definition finds 13% of the living aged 85+ (17% in version 87; almost none here without the drift).

261104 Version 102 applies Josefsson et al.'s rule as their supplement gives it. The check had taken each age group's mean final score and SD from the people tested every time; the rule takes each cohort's average final score from everyone's first test, dropouts' included, and from the rate of change of everyone tested at least twice, weighting each dropout pattern by its share, estimates the SD within each dropout pattern, and classifies everyone tested at least twice, as Betula's 18% maintainers and 13% decliners do. This world gives 16.1% and 15.5% (16.2% and 15.6% in true values); among those tested every time, 16.3% and 15.2%, against 21% and 10%. Only death ends a person's tests here, so 90% of those tested twice are tested every time and correcting for attrition moves little; Betula lost many more. The page's own maintainer definition, absolute until now (at most 0.3 SD lost over the 10 years before 85), is the same rule from 70 to 85 against each person's birth cohort: 21.8% of the living aged 85+. Every SuperAger is one, since the rule judges where 15 years of change leave memory against age peers, not how much was lost.

261104 Later the same day the check took in Betula's older cohorts: those aged 65–85 at the first test were born before the world's first births, so its earliest ten birth years stand in for them, one birth year at each age, as the other checks do. It now gives 16.9% maintainers and 15.1% decliners (17.0% and 15.1% in true values), and 17.5% and 14.5% among those tested every time; 80% of those tested twice are tested every time. In true values maintainers rise with age at the first test, from 14% at 35–39 to 22% at 75–79, as death takes more of those who decline. SuperAgers are now counted everywhere by the Northwestern rule, as the SuperAger check counts them: 14.5% of the dementia-free aged 80+ in true values (14.7% measured once). Hippocampus and memory change together too closely 261014

Version 87 adds a check from Gorbach et al. (2020, Lifebrain consortium): among 748 non-demented people aged 55–90 measured twice about 4 years apart, the yearly change in hippocampal volume correlated 0.21 with the change in episodic memory in APOE ε4 carriers (95% CI 0.06–0.35) and 0.07 in non-carriers (−0.02 to 0.16). Run the same way in this world, with errors of 0.15 mL and 0.4 SD at each visit, the correlations are 0.34 and 0.18: carriers at the top of their interval, non-carriers above theirs. Without the errors they are 0.82 and 0.71, so it is the errors that bring them down to the study's size.

The direction is right: carriers have more AD pathology, which shrinks the hippocampus and lowers memory together. The level is too high because in this world the two changes share nearly all their causes. Normal aging of both scales with one maintenance trait and starts at about the same age (memory's loss at 60, the hippocampal knee near 58), AD, LATE and vascular risk act on both, and memory has no change of its own beyond these. It is the gap that Where are the maintainers? points to: change in memory that differs between people for reasons outside the brain measures and pathology (practice, health, motivation, other systems), which would loosen the coupling and spread change more evenly, giving maintainers in true values. The check is left out of range rather than tuned. Error-corrected estimates of how hippocampal and memory change go together in people free of pathology would set the size of such a part.

261014: version 88 adds that part, a memory drift of each person's own (Where are the maintainers?), sized from the meta-analysis of Tucker-Drob et al. (2019) rather than from this check. Without the errors the correlations fall to 0.74 and 0.58; with them to 0.32 and 0.16, non-carriers at the top of their interval. A larger drift barely moves them further, because at these errors the test error is most of the measured change; drawing the errors afresh moves the non-carriers' figure by about 0.01. The errors assumed are the likelier gap: Lifebrain pooled different memory tests and scanners across six studies, and with errors of 0.55 SD and 0.25 mL at each visit the correlations average about 0.18 and 0.08 over fresh draws of the errors, inside both intervals, with or without the own drift. The study reports no reliabilities, so its errors cannot be checked; if they were that large, the coupling the model builds in fits.

Early dementia passes too quickly 261014

Version 87 staged every life by CDR global. Among people aged 65–84 living with dementia, the stages came out 5% very mild (CDR 0.5), 23% mild, 40% moderate and 33% severe, where the Odense study, which staged a random population sample of those ages the same way, found 41%, 32%, 13% and 14% (Andersen et al. 2010). Version 88 first let the risk of death rise with the stage, using Odense's ratios: 1.82 times the risk of people without dementia when very mild, 2.92 mild, 3.85 moderate and 9.52 severe, where one ratio, 3.5, held before. That cut the severe share, but the early stages stayed rare and short: one person in six skipped very mild and mild dementia, already moderate in the year dementia began, and run Odense's way (everyone aged 65–84 staged once, then followed 14 years) the very mildly demented lived a median of 3.5 years, against Odense's 7.3.

Cognition fell far too fast after diagnosis. After an onset at 80–89, MMSE fell 5.5 points and CDR-SB rose 4.8 points in the first year, where the Cache County study, which followed incident Alzheimer dementia in a population at a mean age of 86, found 1.5 points a year and 1.0 to 1.4 (Tschanz et al. 2011). Two parts of the model did it. Resilience falls with age from 78, doubling pathology's toll every 5 years (Resilience falls with age), and it kept doing so after diagnosis, so in the oldest each further bit of pathology hit four to eight times as hard and the toll already taken kept growing. And CDR-SB climbed steepest right at diagnosis, reaching 9 only 0.5 SD below the threshold, where the crosswalk of Balsis et al. (2015), CDR-SB 4.5 at MMSE 23 and 6.5 at MMSE 20, is less than half as steep.

Version 88 slows both. Aging's late rise takes the place of the fall in resilience (Falling resilience or late-rising aging?): everyone loses a little more each year, an amount that doubles every 6 years (0.0105 SD of episodic memory at 70), so the oldest reach dementia because their level is lower, not because pathology hits them harder; when dementia begins, only the drop in engagement lets aging run a little faster. And CDR-SB climbs in a straight line from 3, reaching 9 at 0.9 SD below the threshold, which matches the crosswalk at MMSE 23 and 20. After an onset at 80–89, MMSE now falls 1.5 points in the first year and 2.0 to 2.3 a year in the next four, and CDR-SB rises 1.4 to 1.7 points a year. That is faster than in Cache County: weighted to its ages at diagnosis, each person's MMSE falls 2.1 points a year over up to 7 years and CDR-SB rises 1.6, against 1.5 and 1.4 (1.8 and 1.3 with the slowing at diagnosis tried first). Among people aged 65–84 with dementia the stages are now 17%, 44%, 27% and 12%. Run Odense's way, median survival is 5.3, 3.8, 3.0 and 2.3 years for very mild, mild, moderate and severe dementia, against 7.3, 4.3, 2.3 and 1.9, and the hazard ratios 2.9, 4.1, 5.7 and 8.3, against 1.8, 2.9, 6.2 and 8.8.

The early stages are still short of Odense's: very mild dementia is 17% of people living with dementia, against 41%, and moderate 27%, against 13%. Odense's very mild stage is a clinician's CDR global of 0.5 in someone with dementia, where the model reads it from CDR-SB 3 to 4 alone, a band of about 1 MMSE point that most cross within the year. Odense staged each case once, when dementia was found, so its ratios carry all the progression after it; applied to the current stage they count some of it twice. Odense did not include people aged 85 or older, so applying its ratios beyond 85 is an extrapolation. Dementia among people 90 and older is 38.9%, within its range (35% published).

The course no longer turns at diagnosis. For onsets at 80–89, global cognition g falls 0.17, 0.18 and 0.20 SD in the three years before and 0.21, 0.23 and 0.25 in the three after, quickening steadily as the late rise doubles; with the fall, slowed at diagnosis, it fell 0.29, 0.39 and 0.50 before and 0.20 to 0.21 after. The late rise has costs of its own. It lowers everyone's memory in the 80s and 90s: pathology-free people aged 90–94 without dementia lose 0.14 SD of memory a year (0.04 with the fall), SuperAgers by the Northwestern rule are 13.7% of the dementia-free aged 80+ (19.2% with the fall), and the page's absolute maintainers 13% (17%). And dementia that begins at 90 or later runs faster than earlier-onset dementia (MMSE 2.1 to 3.4 points a year), where Cache County found, if anything, the reverse: younger onsets declined slightly faster (p = 0.058, and not once people taking dementia drugs were left out). Semantic memory, which falls only from moderate dementia (CDR-SB 9.5), falls later and in fewer people: half reach that point before death, against three in four in version 87.

Every domain drifts on its own 261014

Version 88 first gave episodic memory a drift of its own from 60 (Where are the maintainers?), so memory held still from 40 to 60 before it began to wander, and speed and executive function changed only with aging and pathology: of the differences in 5-year change at 65–75 among people without dementia, 4% for speed and none for executive function were their own. The meta-analysis of Tucker-Drob et al. (2019) finds every domain's change partly its own, with slope communalities of .690 for processing speed, .644 for reasoning, .579 for episodic memory, .562 for working memory and .463 for verbal knowledge, and the share of change common to all domains rising from about 45% at 35 to about 70% at 85.

So the drift now starts at 40 and every cognitive domain has one: each a random step a year, tied to nothing else, the other domains included. Full steps of 0.105 SD a year for episodic memory, 0.04 for speed and 0.065 for executive function, taken as the mean of reasoning and working memory (.603), make each domain's own share of 5-year change at 65–75 41%, 31% and 41%, against 42%, 31% and 40%. Semantic memory shares almost no change with the other domains here among people without dementia, so that share cannot size its drift, which is kept small (0.02).

Steps of one size from 40 made 80–87% of change at 45–55 each domain's own, where the meta-analysis has about half: this world has little shared change before 60. Two remedies were tried against shares extrapolated from it for memory, speed and executive function at four ages (about 52/41/50% at 45–55, 47/36/45% at 55–65, 42/31/40% at 65–75 and 37/26/35% at 75–85). A drift shared by the three fits all four (56/36/51%, 45/36/42%, 42/32/40%, 41/30/38%), but only with steps of 0.10 SD a year shared and 0.07–0.12 SD own, whose differences pile up: early ability then predicts cognition at 77–81 at only 0.44 (published 0.66, range from 0.55), and new dementia at 90–94 falls below its range (8.8% a year). Steps that grow with age leave late life as it was. Growing without end, they made late-life change too much each domain's own (55/36/44% at 75–85). Growing until 60 and full from then, doubling every 7 years, they give 55/59/61%, 42/41/47%, 41/31/41% and 44/29/33%, and early ability predicts cognition at 77–81 at 0.554 (0.545 with steps of one size; 0.553 in other worlds). That is the starting world. A small shared drift (0.03) brings 45–55 closer (56/53/56%) at the cost of that margin (0.547), so it stays a setting, 0 to start.

Memory's spread among dementia-free 50–65-year-olds is 1.06 SD (1.04 with memory's drift alone, from 60). In true values the Betula-like study has 10.9% maintainers and 10.5% decliners (5.6% and 7.0% with memory's drift alone), against 14.9% and 14.3% measured with test error; 31% of the maintainers it finds are maintainers in true values, though 81% truly changed more favorably than their age group. SuperAgers are 11.8% of the living aged 85+. All 14 checks stay in range, the hippocampus–memory correlation in non-carriers on the top edge of its range (0.160).

The puzzle that remains: speed's change at 45–55 is still too much its own (59% against about 41%), and late-life memory change a little (44% against 37%). The growth is fitted to the meta-analysis, not measured. Estimates of shared and domain-specific change at midlife, corrected for practice and test error, would test it.

Where is terminal decline? 261015

Aging's late rise, which gives the oldest old their dementia (Falling resilience or late-rising aging?), falls on everyone alike, and an audit against clinical-pathologic studies found that this gives too much decline to people without pathology and too little to people near death. At version 88's settings, dementia-free 90–94-year-olds whose amyloid, tau, LATE and white matter lesions had not begun lost about 0.15 SD of episodic memory a year, mostly to the late rise, and 54–56% of people who died at 90 or older without AD, LATE or heavy white matter lesions died with dementia, where the 90+ Study found 22% among people with no pathology (Kawas et al. 2015). In the Rush cohorts, among the 573 decedents with at least five assessments (of 856), global cognition declined 0.045 units a year until about 3 years before death and then more than seven times as fast, 0.319 a year (Boyle et al. 2013); here it fell only 1.3 times as fast in the last 3 years of life as in the 7 before. And across all 856, Rush's rates of decline vary between people about as much as their mean (SD 0.11 against a mean of 0.11 units a year), where here they varied about 0.6 of it.

Version 89 cut the late rise to 70% of version 88's for the earliest cohorts' dementia, which eased this only a little: pathology-free 90–94-year-olds lost 0.12 SD of memory a year, 42% of 90+ decedents without the model's pathologies died with dementia (64% of all 90+ decedents, against 54% in the 90+ Study), the last 3 years of life ran 1.4 times as fast as the 7 before, and the spread was 0.63. Cutting it further fails a check: new dementia at 90–94 falls below its range. The papers point to decline that falls on some people rather than everyone and grows steeply with age: a terminal phase tied to mortality, or pathologies the model lacks (hippocampal sclerosis, arteriolosclerosis, microinfarcts, Lewy bodies).

261015 Version 90 adds the first, as a seventh chain, Terminal decline: a phase that begins at a quarter of each person's own risk of death, so that about 30% of deaths after 40 follow one, lasts about 3 years and ends in death, while every cognitive domain falls 0.45 SD a year on average, at a pace that varies widely between people. It takes over decline near death, so the late rise falls to 40% of version 88's, and every check stays in range but dementia at 65–69, as before. Pathology-free 90–94-year-olds now lose 0.10 SD of memory a year; 34% of 90+ decedents without the model's pathologies die with dementia, and 58% of all 90+ decedents; the last 3 years of life run 1.9 times as fast as the 7 before; and the spread is 0.82. The phase is what allows the cut: with the late rise cut alone, new dementia at 90–94 (8.0% a year) and SuperAgers (15.1%) leave their ranges, since the phase gives the very old new cases of dementia and lowers memory in the living near death.

Two gaps remain. Rush's sevenfold is out of reach: in people with a phase, global cognition falls 3.6 times as fast in it as in the 7 years before it began (0.39 against 0.11 SD a year), because AD's toll and what is left of the late rise run steadily in the years before. And the phase falls on people with and without pathology alike, so in the 8 years before death the pathology-free decline at 53% of everyone's average rate (41% in version 89), where Rush finds minimal decline without pathology. There the common pathologies explain under a third of when terminal decline begins or how fast it runs, which leaves most of it, though not all, to a phase like this one; whether terminal decline runs faster with pathology, in people shown to be free of it, would decide whether the phase should depend on pathology. The checks are set in the starting world (seed 7): over seeds 1–6 SuperAgers average 15.7%, just above their range, where in version 89 Mayo's unimpaired 80-year-olds averaged 32.1%, just below theirs.

Clinic and research cohorts are not the population 261020

Huszár et al. (2024) pooled 36 cohorts, nearly all from memory clinics: people with MCI and amyloid converted to dementia at 5.2 times the odds of those without (52.5% against 17.1%), and 74% of the amyloid-positive were positive for CSF p-tau181. In this population world (version 91; MCI, CDR-SB above 0, at 60–80 in the study year, followed 4 years) the odds ratio is 3.0 (40% against 18%), and only 31% of the amyloid-positive with MCI are tau PET-positive (SUVR 1.30). The difference is who is studied: people with MCI who are A+T+ convert as in the clinics (11.0 times the odds of A−T−, against 11.6), and those without amyloid at the clinics' rate (18% against 17%). Recruiting as a memory clinic does, with referrals following memory decline beyond what is usual for one's age, makes 45% of the MCI sample amyloid-positive and 44% of those tau-positive, and the odds ratio 4.0; recruiting research volunteers, better-off, healthier and concerned, makes them 49% and 49%, and 4.6.

Recruitment does not close a second gap. In research cohorts unimpaired A+T+ people progress fast: 57% to MCI or dementia within 5 years with an advanced tau read (Moscoso et al. 2025), and 49–53% within about 3.5 years (Ossenkoppele et al. 2022). Here, among people unimpaired at 60–80 in 2026, 35% do within 5 years, 44% recruited as a memory clinic, and volunteers, whose A−T− progress as rarely as research cohorts' (6% within 5 years, against 3.3% over about 3.5 years in Ossenkoppele et al. and 6.4% over 5 years in Moscoso et al., 95% CI 4.7–8.2), are no faster (35%). Run as a population study at Moscoso et al.'s ages (65–77) in 2018, midway through their years of scanning, with their amyloid cut (24.4 Centiloid), the model gives 36.9% for A+T+ and 10.7% for A−T−. Yet A+T+ memory falls here faster than in the population-based Mayo Clinic Study of Aging (0.19 SD a year at 85, against 0.10–0.13 there; Jack et al. 2019), so a stronger tau toll would fit research cohorts at the population's expense. Whether research volunteers are judged to have MCI at a milder point than population studies, where MCI here comes close to HRS-HCAP's prevalence (19% of people 65 and older here, 22% there; Manly et al. 2022), would decide it.

The same evidence lengthened the amyloid-to-tau lag from 14 to 16 years. At 14, 34% of amyloid-positive people were tau-positive (SUVR 1.25) at 70 and 59% at 85, against the Mayo Clinic Study of Aging's 31% and 52% (Jack et al. 2019), and unimpaired A+T− people turned tau-positive 6.7% a year, against about 5% for medial temporal tau in A4 (Digma et al. 2026); at 16 these are 27%, 52% and 5.3%. The A4 comparison is loose: tau-positive here means SUVR 1.30 or more in the temporal meta-ROI, a neocortical, stage C cut, whereas A4's medial temporal conversion mixes stages B and C. Hippocampal loss before the knee 261023

Version 89 moved the hippocampal knee from 58 to 53, partly to pass the check against the youngest group of Fraser et al. Amyloid's place in the cascade 261024

In the AD cascade amyloid works as a timer. Becoming amyloid positive sets when tau will turn positive, about 16 years later, and tau then follows its own course whatever happens to the plaque; amyloid's own weights are small and act only from tau positivity. Clearing all plaque from the 5,891 people who are amyloid positive and unimpaired at 65–85 in 2026, as in A4, leaves the share who are tau positive 10 years later where it was, 59%, and slows their decline in episodic memory by 0.004 SD a year over 10 years, where removing AD tau slows it by 0.045. In the 760 who are amyloid and tau positive with mild impairment (CDR-SB 0.5–4.5, MMSE 22 or more), clearing plaque slows the rise in CDR-SB by 20.0% over 18 months, a one-time step that falls to 8% by 4 years. Antibodies that clear plaque slowed decline in early AD by about a quarter to a third over 18 months (van Dyck et al. 2023; Sims et al. 2023), which the AD cascade can give only as such a step.

Version 100 keeps the AD cascade as it was and adds the AD cascade expanded in its place, never on with it. The plaque drives tau's rise, all of it to start, and AD's toll, which the AD cascade takes from the tau present, comes along three weighted paths: tau's level, tau's rise and amyloid itself. The last two take the toll the untreated life would have that year, scaled by the share of its rise in tau that happens or of plaque left, so weights adding to 1 leave every untreated life, and every reality check, as the AD cascade has them; the paths part only when amyloid or tau is lowered. Clearing all plaque from the A4-like people then slows memory decline by 0.027 SD a year over 10 years with tau's level carrying the toll (tau stops rising, and 30% rather than 59% are tau positive by then, but the tau already present keeps doing harm), and by 0.048 with tau's rise or amyloid carrying it; removing AD tau slows it by 0.045, 0.047 and 0.003. In the A+T+ people with mild impairment the slowing of CDR-SB at 18 months is 22.2%, 60.5% and 60.5% with plaque cleared, and 27.5%, 57.5% and 0% with AD tau removed.

The reality checks cannot choose the weights, since every split gives the same world, and that is the puzzle left. Amyloid's own path follows the AD cascade's timetable, from the age tau would turn positive, because a toll from amyloid that began at amyloid positivity would change the world and its checks; amyloid's small weights per unit keep the AD cascade's form. Trials that clear plaque before symptoms (AHEAD 3-45; TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 3) and trials that lower tau would tell the paths apart; until they report, the weights are a hypothesis to set rather than a finding.

1. What AgingBrain Simulator is

AgingBrain Simulator is a generative, evolving brain aging simulator in one self-contained HTML file. It simulates a world of people (120,000 lives by default) year by year from age 18 to death, then lets a user run a study in that world and compare what the study concludes with the truth, which the simulation knows. Its stages, the boxes in the left bar, follow a study from start to end: World, Enrollment, Selection, Measurement process, Analysis, Follow-up, Intervention, and Imagine (the slot for a new stage; it is always the last stage, so each new stage goes in just before it). The study year, the year the world is seen in and every study enrolls, is a setting (studyYear, 2026 to start).

It was built to show how who is studied, how they are measured and how the data are analyzed shape conclusions about brain aging: successful aging and SuperAgers, resistance and resilience to Alzheimer's pathology, biomarkers, cognitive decline and dementia.

2. How to work on this file

  • Return one complete, self-contained HTML file, and keep everything that was not asked for exactly as it is.
  • Put a new stage in Imagine's slot, just before Imagine, and keep Imagine as it is as the last stage, so the returned file is ready for another stage (section 7 says how), unless asked to put the stage elsewhere. To change the model itself, follow section 11.
  • Reuse the page's tokens, components and helpers (section 8); add no libraries. The page loads nothing but its fonts (section 3).
  • Never simulate the world again inside a stage: read what the last simulation produced (state.run, section 5).
  • Keep every random draw seeded, so the same settings always give the same result (u01(seed, i) gives a fixed uniform draw for person i).
  • Before handing the file over, open it in a browser and look: light and dark themes, a wide window and a phone-width one (about 390 px), and no errors in the console (section 9).
  • Add a line for every published version to the version history (section 12).
  • The author's conventions: change only what is asked; plain, short labels in sentence case; as little text inside figures as possible; a delivered file ends its name with the date and then the version, as in AgingBrain_Simulator_261010_v80.html (YYMMDD, then v and the published version); a file with a stage added through Imagine ends with the date and its new name instead, as in AgingBrain_Simulator_261023_v97_MA.html (section 7). Download this version of the html application does not know the version number, so it saves AgingBrain_Simulator_YYMMDD.html.

4. The model

Every equation, with the current settings filled in, is on the World page under The properties of this simulated world → How the model works, with its Definitions of every symbol; Reality checks there compare the world with 13 published figures. In brief:

  • Who is born. Each life has a birth year (1926–2008 by default), sex, APOE genotype (ε4 and ε2 alleles), a polygenic score, SES and childhood ability (correlated), an eventual level of schooling (less than high school, high school, some college, or a bachelor's or more: a propensity tied to SES and ability, cut where the person's birth year and sex put the shares at each level, the share who go to college being the Education curve's; E.EDU, E.eduShares), and traits drawn at birth: resistance R (lower amyloid risk), resilience Q (less harm from pathology), maintenance M (slower aging; drawn on its own and set in Aging: maintSD, switched by maintOn), and leanings in sleep and engagement. Cognitive reserve comes from ability and SES. With childhood mortality switched on (child, off to start), some births die before 18, at the rate the Mortality by age 18 curve gives their year of birth and sex, scaled by SES (chSesHR) and childhood ability (chAbilHR); Lives simulated then counts births, and the adults are those who reach 18 (a traced life's t.child is 1 for a death before 18, with t.n 0). With rare variants switched on (rare, rareRate per 100,000 births, off to start), a few people carry autosomal dominant AD (PSEN1, APP or PSEN2; E.RV): their amyloid begins about E.RV.lead years before the age their family's symptoms begin, so their cascade can start before 40 (the one exception to the anchor), their tau builds up faster and higher, and their dementia runs its course in about a decade. A traced life's t.rv is 0 or the gene (1 PSEN1, 2 APP, 3 PSEN2), and t.rvX the family's age at onset.
  • Where each phenotype starts. Levels are set at 40, the anchor: episodic memory, semantic memory, speed of processing, executive function and motivation (in SD), brain volumes (mL) and cortical thickness (mm), with gains for later cohorts. Part of the cohort gain in episodic memory, speed and executive function comes through schooling (eduCog SD for each year of it; within a cohort schooling carries part of the ability and SES weights, whose totals stay abilityCog and sesCog), and the rest is cohortCog. The years from 18 lead into these levels.
  • Seven causal chains change the phenotypes from 40, each with a switch and settings on the World page: Aging (a linear decline from 60 in episodic memory and executive function and from 25 in speed, with cohort gains in level carrying the cross-sectional fall before 60; a late rise in all three that doubles every 6 years; motivation and the cortex at rates that double about every 10 years; the white matter's background load growing 2% a year, with its small toll on cognition; all scaled by maintenance; the hippocampus losing about 0.35% a year until a knee near 53 that differs between people; and each person's own drift in episodic memory, speed, executive function and semantic memory, a random step each year from 40 that grows, doubling every 7 years until it is full at 60 (driftAge, driftDbl, driftTop; full SD 0.105, 0.04, 0.065 and 0.02: memDrift, spdDrift, exeDrift, semDrift; a drift shared by memory, speed and executive function, gDrift, is 0 to start), unrelated to the brain, pathology and one another); Cardiovascular (vascular risk, and white matter lesions that begin at an onset and grow on top of the background load); the AD cascade (amyloid onset, at a risk that begins at 50 and is set to the Mayo Clinic Study of Aging, more likely with ε4 and less with resistance; amyloid rising along an S-curve toward a plateau; tau positive about 16 years later; AD atrophy and cognitive decline from tau positivity, each at a rate set by the tau present), or in its place the AD cascade expanded (version 100, adx, off to start: the AD cascade's settings, with the plaque driving tau's rise, xDrv, and AD's toll split by weights among tau's level, tau's rise and amyloid itself, xwL, xwC, xwA; the two paths beside tau's level take the toll of the untreated course, scaled by the share of its rise in tau that happens or of plaque left, so weights adding to 1 leave every untreated life as the AD cascade has it and only lowering amyloid or tau tells the paths apart; switching either cascade on switches the other off, AD_EXCL, and a file with both keeps the AD cascade); Sleep; Engagement; LATE, the TDP-43 co-pathology of the oldest old; and Terminal decline, a phase of failing health that ends in death, in which every cognitive domain falls fast.
  • Aging's late rise (mem70, spd70, exe70, cogDbl, with Aging): everyone loses a little more episodic memory, speed and executive function each year, an amount that doubles every 6 years (0.0042, 0.005 and 0.0036 SD a year at 70, ten times that at 90; 40% of version 88's from version 90, with terminal decline taking the decline near death, and 70% in version 89, so that the earliest cohorts' dementia fits the checks that count them). It is what lets the oldest old reach the dementia seen in populations, since the AD cascade alone rises too little with age, and because it lowers everyone's cognition alike rather than amplifying pathology, dementia's course runs through diagnosis without a break, much nearer population rates than with the fall. Resilience falling with age (resFallAge, resFallDbl, with Resistance and resilience), which did this from version 75 to 87, is off to start; The properties of this simulated world explains both, under Insights and puzzles (section 10).
  • Terminal decline (term, termShare, termDur, termRate, termSD): a terminal phase begins at 25% of each person's own risk of death (raised by mortality's rise over the phase's length, so the ages at death stay near the life table's) and ends in death; it is set to last 3 years (a Weibull of shape 3) and lasts 2.4 on average, because some people die suddenly before it ends. About 30% of deaths after 40 come after one, the rest suddenly. While it runs, episodic and semantic memory, speed and executive function each fall 0.45 SD a year on average, at a pace that varies widely between people (log SD 1.25). In the Rush cohorts decline speeds up about 3 years before death (Boyle et al. 2013). It takes over part of the decline near death that aging's late rise gave everyone, so the late rise is lower from version 90. A traced life's t.termAt is the age its phase begins (999 if none); the chain's part of a phenotype is term in what explain() returns, the last of E.MPARTS.
  • Dementia begins when global cognition g = (episodic memory + speed + executive)/3 falls to −2.3 SD or below after a fall of at least 1.2 SD since 40. MMSE follows g (about 24 at the threshold). CDR Sum of Boxes is 0 far from the criteria, 0.5–2.5 within about 0.8 SD of them, 3 at diagnosis, and from there rises in a straight line, 9 at 0.9 SD below the threshold, to 18 (cdr9, cdrLin; the slope of the MMSE crosswalk of Balsis et al. 2015). After an onset in the 80s, MMSE falls about 1.5 points in the first year and 2 to 2.4 a year after, and CDR-SB rises 1.4 to 1.7 points a year, somewhat faster than in the Cache County study (1.5 and 1.4). CDR global follows from it by its usual bands (E.cdrGlobal(sb): 0; 0.5 for 0.5–4; 1 for 4.5–9; 2 for 9.5–15.5; 3 for 16–18), and so does the clinical stage (E.stageOf(sb, dementia): 0 unimpaired, 1 MCI, CDR 0.5 without dementia, 2 dementia, which is very mild at CDR 0.5 and mild, moderate and severe at 1, 2 and 3).
  • Biomarkers. Amyloid PET in Centiloid (positive at 20), plasma pTau217 (rises with amyloid, then with tau, and a little with age), tau PET as SUVR (positive at 1.30; a small rise with age from 50, plus AD tau), white matter hyperintensities (mL).
  • Death follows a Gompertz curve, raised by vascular risk and by dementia, the more the further dementia has gone (×1.82, 2.92, 3.85 and 9.52 at CDR 0.5, 1, 2 and 3, from the Odense study; demHR, demHR1, demHR2, demHR3); with terminal decline on, part of that risk (termShare) leads into a terminal phase, which then ends in death; a life's simulation ends at death.
  • Cohort effects (education, mortality by age 18 and vaccinations by year of birth; depression, war, smoking, pandemic, folic acid, screen time and the era of AI by calendar year), each with a curve for women and one for men, are drawn on the World page and saved with the settings in state.cohort (for each exposure, w and m, 21 values each). Education is linked to lives: simulate() passes its curve to the engine, where it sets each person's eventual schooling (section 11). Mortality by age 18 is linked too: passed the same way, it sets who dies before 18 when Childhood mortality is on. The others are not yet linked to how lives unfold.
  • SuperAgers and maintainers (saThr, mtSD, in their own box): a SuperAger by the Harrison and Rogalski rule (Harrison et al. 2012; Rogalski et al. 2013), one test in the study year: aged 80 or older without dementia, with episodic memory at or above the mean of the dementia-free aged 56–66 (plus saThr), and executive function and semantic memory each no more than 1 SD below the mean of the dementia-free in the same 5-year age group (E.nwRule: S.saRef, the bars, which traced lives take as E.trace's fourth argument and Selection's SuperAger strategy uses with a memory margin of its own, and each living person's w.sup); a maintainer by the rule of Josefsson et al. (2012) from 70 to 85 (a final score, memory at 70 plus 15 years of the slope through tests every 5 years, at least 1 SD above the average final score of the person's 5-year birth cohort, which counts those who die before 85: S.mtRef, which traced lives read as k.mtRef; S.mtSY and each living person's w.mtF apply the rule to the 15 years before the study year, for Selection's maintainer strategy). The engine counts SuperAgers among the dementia-free aged 80+ (S.sup80p, S.supNoAD80p, S.supAD80p) and maintainers among the living aged 85+ (S.mt85p, S.both85p), in true values, and marks each traced life (t.exceptional, t.maint, t.patSY, AD pathology in the study year); the highlights use these. The maintainer check applies Josefsson's rule to a Betula-like study, in true values and with test error, with dropout by death and the world's earliest ten birth years standing in at the ages its births do not reach (S.chk.jMaint, jDecl, jMaintT, jDeclT; among those tested every time jMaint4, jDecl4, jMaint4T, jDecl4T), and compares it with Betula's attrition-corrected figures for everyone tested at least twice. A second check, after Gorbach et al. (2020), correlates the change per year in hippocampal volume with that in episodic memory over two visits 4 years apart, in APOE ε4 carriers and non-carriers (S.chk.gorE4, gorNon; in true values gorE4T, gorNonT).

5. Settings, and the data after a simulation

Settings. state holds every setting as plain values: state.p (the world's parameters; state.p.seed is the world number and state.p.N the number of lives), state.cohort, state.enr (Enrollment), state.sel (strat, the strategy in use, q, every strategy's settings, and meas, whether it selects on measured values), state.ms (Measurement process: each variable's error, validity and practice) and state.mx (its missed visits and dropout), state.an (Analysis and Follow-up), state.iv (Intervention), state.panel (the open stage), and what the World page shows (vars, ageLo, ageHi, frac, hl, pin…). Save writes all of them to a JSON file (keepSnapshot) and Load puts them back (keepRead checks each value, keepApply applies them).

The world. state.run = { p, k, agg, S } once a world has been simulated (null before); a stage draws nothing until it is set. A new world (new settings or New world) replaces it.

Everyone alive in the study year (SY() gives it for the world on screen; 2026 to start): const w = state.run.agg.w26, a set of columns with one row per person, r from 0 to w.n − 1: w.id[r] (the life's number, 0-based; shown as Life id + 1), w.age[r], w.fem[r] (1 for women), w.geno[r] (an index into E.GENO: 0 ε2/ε2, 1 ε2/ε3, 2 ε2/ε4, 3 ε3/ε3, 4 ε3/ε4, 5 ε4/ε4), w.ses[r], w.abil[r], w.dem[r] (1 with dementia), w.ms[r] (1 with vascular risk), w.m5[r], w.m10[r] and w.m15[r] (episodic memory 5, 10 and 15 years before), w.c5[r], w.c10[r] and w.c15[r] (CDR-SB then), w.edu[r] (eventual schooling, an index into E.EDU.levels), w.rv[r] (a rare variant: 0 none, 1 PSEN1, 2 APP, 3 PSEN2).

  • wv(w, r, key): a phenotype's true value in the study year. anVal(w, r, key) also takes "age", "ses", "abil" and "e4" (the number of ε4 alleles).
  • anObs(w, r, key): the value as a study would measure it, with the error and validity set in Measurement process (msAt does one measurement; its last argument is the practice the earlier tests left, which msExpo works out and anRecs passes for each visit).
  • Phenotype keys, with VMETA[key] = { label, unit, d (decimals), fixed?, ticks?, thr?, log? } (log: pTau217 is drawn on a log scale on the World page, and its unit reads "pg/mL, log", shown without ", log"). ANV[key] holds only label, a cleaned unit, d, fixed and ticks for the variables the analysis pages offer (the phenotypes and age, ses, abil, e4); it has no thr or log, so those pages draw pTau217 on a linear axis. The keys: mmse (points), cdr (CDR Sum of Boxes, points), mem (episodic memory), sem (semantic memory), spd, exe, mot (SD), wba (whole brain), tiv (intracranial volume), hip (hippocampus), wmh (all mL), ctx (cortical thickness, mm), amy (amyloid PET, Centiloid), ptau (plasma pTau217, pg/mL on the scale of the ALZpath assay), tau (tau PET, SUVR).
  • True values keep their scales' steps: MMSE in whole points, CDR-SB in half points. Cognition is in SD. Behavioral measures (episodic and semantic memory, speed, executive function, motivation) level off at their extremes: a fall from the level at 40 bends at half of cogFloor (6 SD) and approaches it, so they plateau in severe dementia, while dementia, MMSE and CDR-SB follow the fall itself. WMH is very skewed. In Measurement process, validity works on the log scale for pTau217 and WMH (MS_LOG), but the error is added in each variable's own units (for pTau217, as a percentage of the value: MS_PCT), and measured values stay above a floor (0.01 for pTau217 and WMH).
  • The convention for measured values: when a page's measurement error or practice effects are on, the sample is shown with them and everyone alive stays at true values (anValNote says so under the figure; practice only touches visits after the first). Follow it, or say clearly when a stage does otherwise.

The study sample: const R = runSelection() returns { st, q, lo, hi, world, elig, meet, sel }: the strategy in use (st.title, st.color), its settings, its ages, and lists of rows of w: everyone alive at those ages, those who seek to enroll, those who meet the criteria, and those selected. With Selection's Measurement error switch on (meas), meet is who meets the criteria as measured once on entry, meetT who meets them in true values, and mis who is misclassified (inT marks meetT; missed; counts wrong and selWrong). It is cached, so call it freely. groupStats(w, rows) summarizes any list of rows and statTable([{ title, g }]) lays such summaries out as a table.

A whole life: const t = E.trace(i, state.run.p, state.run.k, state.run.S.saRef) for life number i returns { i, n, tr, b (birth year), female, geno, n4 (ε4 alleles), ses, abil, edu, eduY (schooling level and years), death (exact age), demAge, aPos, tPos (the first whole year of age with dementia, amyloid positive and tau positive, since a life is simulated a year at a time; 999 if never), msOn, wOn, lOn, termAt (onsets of vascular risk, lesions, LATE and a terminal phase at exact ages, not whole years; 999 if never), exceptional (a SuperAger in the study year), maint (a maintainer), patSY (AD pathology in the study year), traits }. A phenotype at age a: t.tr[E.IX[key] * t.n + (a − E.AGE0)], for ages 18 to 18 + t.n − 1. A fifth argument { a0, kind, x } lives the life with an intervention from age a0 (kind "aging", "ad" or "cv" with x a fraction, "amy" or "tau" with x the fraction of plaque or of AD tau removed, or "sleep" or "eng" with x in SD). E.lifecourse(i, p, k) gives each year's processes too, and E.explain(i, p, k, age) each process's share of each phenotype at one age.

Follow-up: fuOf(w, r) is a participant's future from entry, anVisits(q) the visit times for a { yrs, step }, and anRecs(w, rows, ys, E.IX[key], error, fuOf, practice, missing) each participant's values at those visits, with the measurement error and the practice effects when those are true, and their slope; with missing true (pass mxOn("fu") or mxOn("iv")) it applies the missed visits and dropout set in Measurement process (mxStatus), keeps only the visits seen, and gives at[k] (where visit k sits in pts, −1 if not seen; read it with anAt(p, k)), nl (visits lived to), dr (the visit the participant left the study at, −1 if never) and nm (visits missed). ivOf(w, r) is the same future under the Intervention's settings.

Engine constants: E.STUDY_YEAR 2026 (the starting study year; the world's own is state.run.p.studyYear), E.AGE0 18, E.ANCHOR 40, E.AGEMAX 110, E.NV and E.IX[key] (the phenotypes and their order), E.GENO, E.A_POS 20 and E.T_POS 1.30 (positivity), E.SRC (the years and cohorts the reality checks count), E.DEFAULTS (every world setting). state.run.S holds the world's summaries, among them refMean and refSD, episodic memory's mean and SD in people aged 50–65 without dementia, and saRef, the bars a SuperAger clears.

6. The stages as built

  • World: Baseline determinants (who is born and where each phenotype starts, by setting boxes, and the cohort curves); Causal chains (a banner that scales the chosen chains' combined effect with a dial and draws the chosen phenotypes over one reference life, a woman with every baseline factor at its mean who does not die, so terminal decline is left out of it, aggRender(); a card per chain with its switch, settings, a causal diagram and the selected life's values in blue); headline numbers; Expressed phenotypes (six plots of sampled lives; click a line to select a life, see its values and its life in detail); and The properties of this simulated world (prevalence by age, what makes people differ in episodic memory, the average person over a lifetime, How the model works, Reality checks, AgingBrain Simulator version history, Insights and puzzles from decisions required to get the model to work).
  • Enrollment: how people are recruited (REC: as a population study, a memory clinic or research volunteers; state.enr.rec) and the share of each group that comes forward (ENR, seekers()); selection draws only from them.
  • Selection: twelve strategies (SEL): Random; Nondemented; Age peers (percentile); Younger-adult norms (SuperAger); Own earlier performance (maintainer); Expected for pathology (resilience); Expected for age (resistance); Expected for genotype (escape); Amyloid+ and unimpaired (A4); Tau+ and unimpaired (tau PET); Mild impairment (MCI); and Dementia. Each takes ages, a number of participants, a share of women and its own criteria, and draws at random within sex from those who meet them, using true values in the study year or, with Selection's Measurement error switch on, values measured once on entry, as Analysis shows them (selMeet; the norms stay in true values). The clinical stages are criteria too: Nondemented can keep the unimpaired, or the unimpaired and MCI; MCI can be the stage itself (CDR 0.5 without dementia) or bands of CDR-SB and MMSE; Dementia can be limited to stages of CDR global (DEM_STAGES). Analysis, Follow-up and Intervention can switch strategy at the top of their pages (the parts to reuse are in section 8).
  • Measurement process: for each variable, the error of one measurement at 70 and how it scales with age, CDR-SB and sex, its validity (the correlation with what it should measure), and a practice effect (the gain at the first retest, the ceiling it approaches, and the half-life over which it fades between tests; none for brain and biomarker measures); the page shows test–retest r, MDC95 and r with the true value, and the practice model of the chosen variable. Analysis, Follow-up and Intervention each have two toggles, one to add the measurement error and one the practice effects (on Analysis, where everyone is measured once, practice is greyed out). Below, missed visits and dropout (state.mx): for each, a rate for a 70-year-old of average SES and factors for age, lower SES, decline in global cognition since entry and dementia that begins after entry, none to start, applied in Follow-up and Intervention (switches fu and iv), with who is seen at each of the Follow-up's visits.
  • Analysis: the sample in the study year, by age, as two age groups, or one variable against another; adjustments for age, sex, head size and ε4; groups by color; the same fit in everyone alive at those ages; click a participant for their life in detail.
  • Follow-up: the sample seen again at regular visits for up to 20 years, as trajectories or as change against a value at entry; people leave by dying, and, when they are set, by dropping out, and they miss visits.
  • Intervention: the sample randomized at entry, one arm given an intervention on one chain, or one that lowers amyloid or AD tau; the trial's estimate is set against the true effect, from each treated person's life lived without it.

8. Drawing and components

  • Charts are SVG written in code, with no libraries: sized to their box (box.clientWidth), class="wp-svg", margins near { l: 46, r: 10, t: 8, b: 34 }. Grid lines wp-gl, tick labels wp-ax, axis titles wp-axt; niceTicks(lo, hi, n) and tickLabel(v, step) make the ticks. Redraw on resize (section 7).
  • Color comes from tokens in style attributes, as in style="fill:var(--mx0)", so both themes work: --accent (orange) and --pair-b for two groups; --mx0…--mx9 for categories (each selection strategy has one: R.st.color); --dementia; --super; --gr0…--gr4 for a graded scale; --context for background points; --ink, --ink-2, --muted for text.
  • Tooltips: a <div class="tip" hidden> in the chart's box and wpTip(box, html, x, y) (wpTip(box, null) hides it). Legends: div.legend of <span><i class="lg-dot"></i>Label</span> (also lg-line, lg-sq).
  • Tables: table.wp-tbl with numbers in td.num, inside div.wp-tw so a wide table scrolls on its own.
  • Controls: rows label.fld (a span label, then a select.sel or input type="number"); label.fld.chk for a checkbox; div.wp-seg buttons with aria-pressed for a choice of views; .switch for on and off; .btn, .btn.quiet, .btn.sm for buttons; p.hint for notes; section.box with h3 and p.sub. A banner that opens is details.sect.detail with a summary (see this one).
  • Parts the analysis pages share: the strategy menu at the top of a page (div.an-src, filled and wired by stratPick(id, render) and kept in step, its edge in the strategy's color, by stratSync(id, R)). Call stratPick once, from the stage's build function, since each call adds a listener. A change of strategy there is not local: it sets the shared state.sel.strat, so Selection and every page with the menu switch with it, and it also clears Selection's chosen variable (state.sel.y) and rebuilds Selection's controls (buildSelCtl()) before it calls render. The "Sample: …" line, anSampleLine(R), writes into #<prefix>-sample for the page prefix in ANP ($a(k) is $("#" + ANP + "-" + k)). ANP is one shared variable, which each page sets as its render begins (renderMeasure "ms", renderAnalysis "an", renderFollow "fu", renderInterv "iv"), so a new stage that uses $a(), anSampleLine or another part that calls it must set ANP to its own prefix at the start of its render and give its elements ids with that prefix. And the measurement toggles, div.an-tgs with a button.an-tg (aria-pressed) for measurement error and one for practice effects, whose markup anTgl(q, errorKey, practiceKey, attr) makes (the buttons carry their keys of q in attr; with no practice key that button is greyed out); a stage wires them with anTglWire(box, attr, q, rebuild, render) and its Measurement process link (data-go) itself. See buildAnalysis and renderAnalysis.
  • Statistics: anMean(values) ({ n, m, sd, lo, hi }: mean, SD and 95% CI), ivDiff(a, b) (the difference of two anMean results by Welch's t: { d, lo, hi, p }, where d is the raw difference, not Cohen's d; null when a group has fewer than two people), olsFit(X, y) and olsFull(X, y) (with standard errors), anFitG(rows, xf, yf, cov) (a slope with adjustments, anCov builds them), pearson(x, y), tP(t, df) and tCrit(df), normInv(q).
  • Text: fmtInt(n), pct(x, decimals), sg(v, decimals) (with a sign), anNf(v, d) (with d decimals, by default three significant figures from anDigits(v), as the analysis tables show numbers; print a CI at its estimate's decimals) and anPf(p), fmtVal(VMETA[key], v), anLc(label) (a label in mid-sentence), lifeName(t) ("Life 12,345"), and esc(text) before putting text into HTML.
  • Layout: two columns fold into one below 1000 px wide; at 390 px nothing may scroll sideways. Tooltips do not wrap, so keep them short; a wide table scrolls inside div.wp-tw, so at phone width give it few columns or set it out as a list.

10. Capture of insights and unresolved puzzles

When a decision needed to make the model work teaches something, or leaves a question the model cannot yet answer, it is captured as an entry under Insights and puzzles from decisions required to get the model to work (World page, The properties of this simulated world; details#wp-struct). This capture of insights and unresolved puzzles keeps a dated record of why the model is built as it is, so the reasons survive later changes.

  • Each entry is an expandable bullet, details.ins with an id, whose title is a short name followed by the date it was captured as YYMMDD in span.ins-d: for example Resilience falls with age 261010.
  • The date stays fixed: it records when the insight or puzzle was captured, not when it was last touched, so an entry is never re-dated.
  • The body says what was found and why the decision was needed, what was decided, and its limits and complexities. A puzzle says what remains unexplained, what was tried, and what would settle it.
  • Entries are added, not rewritten. When later work changes the understanding, add a new dated entry that names the earlier one; the earlier one keeps its text, with at most a dated line at its end pointing to the new entry. New entries go after the last.

11. Changing the model

A change to the model touches the engine (the first script, ENGINE) and the page together. Work through these in order.

  1. Only state.p and two cohort curves reach the engine. simulate() hands E.prep, E.newAgg and E.runChunk a copy of state.p with the Education curve added as eduW and eduM and the Mortality by age 18 curve as ch18W and ch18M (simParams()); nothing else in state is passed. A setting kept anywhere else never changes a life. The other cohort curves are the example: drawn from COHORT, kept in state.cohort and saved, they never reach the engine, which is why section 4 calls them not yet linked. Education shows how one was linked: its defaults live in the engine (E.EDU), the card passes the curve on, a change to it simulates the world again (eduChanged()), and Load compares it too (eduSame); Mortality by age 18 follows it (E.CH18, ch18Changed(), ch18Same). To make an input count, keep it in state.p as plain numbers or pass it to the engine explicitly in the same way, and use it there.
  2. A new setting. Add it to E.DEFAULTS with a comment on what it does and its unit; derive anything fixed from it in prep() (as k.…); and use it in setup() (a person's draws and levels at 40) or live() (each year of a life). Then give it a control. In a Baseline determinants box, add { k, label, min, max, step, fmt } to that box's params in GROUPS; its range goes into PDEF by itself. In a causal chain, add a row [key, label, unit, pv] to a group of the chain's entry in PROCS (pv(X, P), optional, gives the selected life's value in blue) and its range and step to the Object.assign(PDEF, …) list, since numRow needs them. A switch is a boolean in E.DEFAULTS, named by the chain's or box's toggle.
  3. A new link or chain. Besides the engine's term and its PROCS row, update DGM[id] for the chain. It draws the chain's causal diagram and feeds the aggregate-effects banner: say (the comparison in words), with and without (what the reference life is given with the chain and without it), off (the settings that switch the chain off), nodes (with zero, where given, the settings that silence a node's links), drivers, rows (which phenotypes each node acts on) and out (its links into other chains). A new chain also needs its own PROCS entry, modelled on the others (id, title, sub, toggle, chart, flow, status, groups; optionally a note, shown at the top of its settings), its switch, and a drawing for its small chart in SPECS[chart]. A chain that stands in for another, as the AD cascade expanded does for the AD cascade, goes last in PROCS so the other cards keep their places, and is paired in AD_EXCL so that switching one on switches the other off; the engine and keepRead also keep only the first of the pair when both are set.
  4. Keep the accounting exact. A process that moves a phenotype adds its part to the breakdown that live() records for explain() (the parts Life in detail shows) and, for episodic memory, to MPARTS in memoryShares, so the parts still add up to the whole. Behavioral measures pass through the floor (flr, flrParts); dementia, MMSE and CDR-SB use the fall before it.
  5. Random draws. Each person's draws are the array z, NZ values from draw(): uniforms first, then normals in pairs. A new draw goes at the end, by raising NZ by 2. Never reorder or reuse an index, or every person changes. Draws needed every year, like the own drifts' steps, come from hz(seed, life, q + age) instead (q 100 for episodic memory, 300 speed, 500 executive, 700 semantic memory, 900 the shared drift), which leaves z alone.
  6. Say what changed. Add the new term to How the model works (wpModelHtml) and its symbols to Definitions, and update section 4 of this guide. A new reality check is a row in wpChecks, with its value computed in finish() (S.chk) over the people its source studied: its years or birth cohorts, or, for people born before the world's first births, the earliest ten birth years (E.SRC, E.EARLY; the …All values pool every year of every life).
  7. Old settings files. Save writes every key of state.p, and keepRead reads back every key of E.DEFAULTS (a missing or out-of-range value takes the starting one), so a new setting needs nothing more, unless the change alters what an older file's settings mean. Then add a migration to keepRead in the manner of AD_OLD, AG_OLD, AG_V74 and CAL_V88 (a file left at the old starting values takes the new model; one with changes of its own runs as it was saved), return a note from keepRead and add its words to the list keepApply returns (the load message shows them), and raise KEEP_V.
  8. Check it. The reality checks in range (11 of 12 to start: dementia at 65–69 runs below its range, since the model has no early-onset dementias besides AD and amyloid follows the Mayo population; Mayo's amyloid-positive unimpaired 80-year-olds and new dementia at 90–94 sit near the bottom of their ranges), the headline numbers, and, for a change meant to leave existing worlds alone, the same numbers as before with the new setting at its neutral value. Then record the decision under Insights and puzzles (section 10).

12. Keeping the version history

Every published version adds one line to AgingBrain Simulator version history (World page, The properties of this simulated world; ol#vh-list), in the same change that is published, so the record runs without gaps.

  • Where. At the top of the list, which runs newest first and shows about the ten latest, scrolling for older ones. The number is one more than the line below it, except for a stage added through Imagine (below).
  • How brief. One line of at most about 15 words, saying what changed for someone using the simulator rather than how it was done: li.vh, with the date in span.vh-d, the number in span.vh-v (as v82) and the text in span.vh-t. A major addition (a new stage, causal chain, model mechanism or banner, or a capability such as Save and Load) is li.vh.maj: a short bold title, then at most three lines in span.vh-x.
  • Dating. The date of publication as YYMMDD, in the publisher's local time. The second and later versions published the same day add .2, .3 and so on: 261012, 261012.2, 261012.3. Exact times are not recorded.
  • A stage added through Imagine. Its version keeps the number it was built from and adds the stage's tag (section 7): one line, as v97_MA, dated the day it was built and saying which stage it adds, with its originator if known. How it was built is in the stage's banner; revisions asked for in the same conversation are part of that version and add no line.
  • Originator. Optional: the initials or name of the person who made or asked for the change, in span.vh-by at the end of the line. The author's versions are marked RLB. Leave it empty when unknown.
  • Never rewritten. A line is not edited or re-dated afterward; a correction is a new line. What a change teaches about the model goes under Insights and puzzles (section 10).
